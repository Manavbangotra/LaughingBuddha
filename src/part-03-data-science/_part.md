## What this part is for

Parts I and II gave you tools. This part is about the discipline of pointing
them at real data and getting an answer that is actually true.

That turns out to be mostly a matter of not being fooled. The mathematics in
{{part:1}} is unambiguous and the code in {{part:2}} either runs or does not.
Data is neither. It arrives with missing values that mean three different
things, correlations that reverse when you look closer, a join key that is not
unique, and a target column that was populated after the event you are
predicting. None of these announce themselves. All of them produce a
plausible-looking number.

So Part III is organised around failure. Each chapter teaches a technique and
then spends real time on how that technique produces confident wrong answers,
because recognising a wrong answer is the skill that transfers.

## What is here

- **Chapters 21–24** — the ground floor. What data science actually is, getting
  data in, cleaning it, and looking at it before modelling it.
- **Chapters 25–26** — the causal core. Why correlation misleads, and the one
  reliable method for establishing that something caused something else.
- **Chapters 27–28** — building features, and the pathologies that make good
  validation scores meaningless.
- **Chapters 29–30** — two problem shapes that break the standard assumptions:
  ordered data, and data generated by the system's own recommendations.

## The uncomfortable part

Two chapters here are more important than the rest, and both are about being
wrong rather than about doing something.

{{ch:ds-causation}} is the most consequential chapter in the part. Almost every
data-science claim that turns out to be false is a causal claim supported only
by an association. Simpson's paradox {{cite:simpson1951}} — where a relationship
present in every subgroup reverses on pooling — is not a curiosity; it is the
clearest available demonstration that **the data does not contain enough
information to determine the right answer**, and that you need to know how it
was generated.

{{ch:ds-leakage}} is the most consequential in practice. Leakage
{{cite:kaufman2012}} is the standard reason a model with excellent validation
scores fails in production, and it is almost always introduced by a step that
looked entirely reasonable at the time.

If you read only two chapters of this part properly, read those.

## How AI has changed this

The book's brief asks how modern AI is changing data science, and the honest
answer is more specific than either of the usual ones.

**What has genuinely been automated is the execution.** An agent will write the
SQL, generate a first-pass exploratory analysis, propose feature
transformations, and sweep model families — competently, in seconds, at a
standard that would have taken a capable analyst hours. Frameworks now attempt
whole workflows end to end rather than isolated snippets
{{cite:automind2025}}. {{maturity:EMERGING}}

**What has not been automated is the judgement.** Deciding which question is
worth asking. Knowing that the `signup_date` column was backfilled in March and
is unreliable before then. Recognising that the treatment group is younger
because of how the rollout was staged. Noticing that a join added 3% more rows
than it should have. Deciding whether a two-week lift is a real improvement or a
novelty effect.

Those are not harder versions of the automated tasks. They are a different kind
of task, and they depend on knowing things that are not in the dataset.

> IMPORTANT: The practical consequence runs opposite to intuition. Because
> generating a plausible analysis is now nearly free, the *checking* is worth
> proportionally more than it used to be. An agent will produce a confounded
> comparison, a leaking feature, or a join that silently duplicated rows just as
> readily as a human — and much faster. The failure modes in this part are the
> content that has appreciated in value, not depreciated.

{{part:20}} returns to this properly, once agents have been covered.

## What this part deliberately does not cover

Data infrastructure at scale — Spark, warehouses, orchestration — which belongs
in {{part:23}}. Formal causal inference beyond confounding and randomisation:
instrumental variables, difference-in-differences and the do-calculus are a
book, and {{ch:ds-causation}} teaches enough to stop you making the common error
and to read further. Classical time-series identification, because what
transfers to {{part:7}} is the ordering constraint rather than Box-Jenkins.
Deep recommenders, which need the embeddings of {{part:11}} first.

## What you should be able to do at the end

Interrogate a dataset's provenance before trusting it. Clean data with a
documented reason for every decision. Produce an exploratory analysis that
surfaces problems rather than confirming expectations. Explain why a correlation
does not establish causation, and construct a case where the subgroup and pooled
answers disagree. Design an A/B test with adequate power and analyse it without
peeking. Engineer features that will exist at prediction time. Find leakage in
someone else's pipeline. Validate a time-ordered model without training on the
future. Build a recommender and explain its feedback loop.

The assignment at the end of this part requires all of it on one messy dataset.
